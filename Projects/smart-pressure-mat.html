Smart Pressure Mat & Pressure Sensing System

A pressure-sensing system developed to measure and visualize pressure distribution, with applications in patient monitoring and pressure-ulcer prevention.

Project Overview

The Smart Pressure Mat is an embedded sensing system designed to measure pressure distribution across a surface and translate the sensor measurements into a visual representation of pressure. The project explores how low-cost pressure sensors and microcontrollers can be combined to create a system for monitoring pressure distribution.

The project began as a pressure sensor data logger and was progressively expanded into a 2×2 sensor grid. This development established the foundation for a larger pressure-sensing mat intended for healthcare applications, including monitoring patient pressure distribution and supporting pressure-ulcer prevention strategies. Inspiration for this project came from being a fellow of the UConn NurseEng Fellowship

The Problem

Prolonged pressure on the same areas of the body can contribute to the development of pressure ulcers, particularly for patients with limited mobility. Monitoring pressure distribution can help caregivers identify areas experiencing elevated or sustained pressure.

Existing pressure-monitoring systems can be expensive or difficult to adapt to specific applications. This project explored the development of a lower-cost sensing system capable of measuring pressure at multiple locations and representing the measurements in an intuitive way.

Motivation

I was interested in developing a project that combined electrical engineering, embedded systems, and healthcare technology to address a real-world problem.

The project also provided an opportunity to learn how individual sensors can be integrated into a larger sensing system and how raw sensor measurements can be processed into information that is easier for a user to interpret.

My Role

I contributed to the design and development of the pressure sensing system, including sensor integration, microcontroller programming, data collection, and development of the pressure visualization.

I worked with force-sensitive resistor (FSR) sensors and an Arduino Uno microcontroller to collect pressure measurements. I also helped develop the system from an individual sensor into a 2×2 sensing grid.

System Architecture

The system uses force-sensitive resistors to measure pressure at individual locations. The sensor signals are read by a microcontroller and processed into pressure measurements that can be represented spatially.

Hardware

The prototype uses force-sensitive resistor (FSR) sensors to detect changes in applied pressure. The sensors are connected to an Arduino microcontroller, which serves as the primary processing unit for collecting the sensor measurements.

The initial prototype used a 2×2 sensor arrangement, creating four sensing locations that could be monitored simultaneously, but future iterations include introducing a multiplexer and switching to an ESP32 FireBeetle microcontroller.

Software & Data Collection

The system was programmed using Arduino-compatible C/C++ to collect measurements from the pressure sensors. The microcontroller continuously reads the sensor values and associates each measurement with its corresponding location in the sensor grid.

The collected measurements can then be processed to represent the relative pressure at each sensing location. This provides the foundation for generating a pressure heat map, allowing pressure distribution to be interpreted spatially rather than as a collection of individual numerical readings.

Prototype Development

The system was developed incrementally rather than attempting to build the complete sensing mat immediately. Initial development focused on reading and understanding the output of an individual FSR sensor.

After establishing reliable sensor measurements, the system was expanded into a 2×2 grid. This allowed pressure to be measured at multiple locations simultaneously and introduced the additional engineering challenges associated with multiplexing, organizing sensor data, and representing the measurements spatially.

The 2×2 prototype serves as a proof of concept for scaling the system to a larger sensor array.

Testing

Testing focused on verifying that the pressure sensors produced measurable and repeatable changes in their output when pressure was applied at different locations.

The individual sensors were tested before being incorporated into the 2×2 grid. The completed grid was then tested by applying pressure to different sensing locations and observing whether the corresponding sensor measurements changed as expected.

Results

The prototype successfully demonstrated the ability to collect pressure measurements from multiple FSR sensors using a microcontroller-based system. Expanding from a single sensor to a 2×2 grid demonstrated that the system could distinguish pressure measurements across multiple locations.

The collected sensor data provided the foundation for a real-time pressure heat map, allowing the system to move beyond individual sensor readings toward a spatial representation of pressure distribution. The heat map is currently one of the next things to be added.

Challenges

One challenge was working with FSR sensors, which do not provide a direct measurement of pressure in standard units. Their output depends on factors such as sensor characteristics, how force is applied, and the surrounding mechanical setup.

Expanding from a single sensor to multiple sensors also introduced additional complexity in wiring, sensor selection, data organization, and ensuring that measurements could be associated with the correct physical location.

Another challenge was determining how to translate raw sensor measurements into a visualization that would be intuitive for a user to interpret.

What I Learned

This project gave me hands-on experience with embedded systems, sensors, microcontrollers, and data acquisition. I learned how to move from reading a single sensor to integrating multiple sensors into a larger system.

I also learned that developing a hardware system involves much more than writing code. Sensor behavior, wiring, physical construction, signal interpretation, and software all have to work together for the system to produce useful information.

Most importantly, the project strengthened my interest in developing engineering systems that combine hardware, software, and healthcare applications.

Future Improvements

The next major improvement would be scaling the sensor array beyond the initial 2×2 prototype. A larger array would provide greater spatial resolution and allow pressure distribution across a larger area to be monitored.

Additional work could also improve sensor calibration and convert raw sensor readings into more meaningful pressure measurements. Improving the mechanical construction of the sensing layers would also help create a more durable and consistent prototype.

Future versions could incorporate wireless communication, battery optimization, real-time data visualization, and automated alerts when sustained high-pressure regions are detected.

Project Takeaway

This project allowed me to develop a complete embedded sensing system from the sensor level through data collection and visualization. Starting with an individual pressure sensor and progressing to a 2×2 sensing grid taught me how hardware, software, and system design must work together to solve a real-world engineering problem.

The project also demonstrated how relatively simple sensing hardware can serve as the foundation for a larger healthcare technology system.
